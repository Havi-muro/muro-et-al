## Why this matters

A farmer manages a farm, not a plot: the same mowing regime, the same herd and the same fertiliser plan are applied across fields that can be kilometres apart. Vegetation, however, is surveyed in small square plots, and almost everything we know about how management changes plant communities comes from those squares. The scale at which decisions are taken and the scale at which they are measured do not match, and a subsidy paid per hectare of farm cannot be checked against a measurement made per plot.

Plant functional diversity, the range of strategies that plants use to grow and survive, matters because it underpins how a grassland responds to drought, to cutting and to grazing. Whether that diversity, and its response to management, looks the same at the scale of a farm is the question this paper asks.

</div>

## What we did

We worked in semi-natural grasslands of three German regions of the Biodiversity Exploratories: Schorfheide-Chorin, Hainich-Dün and Schwäbische Alb. In each region, 50 permanent plots were surveyed annually for seven years, and their farms recorded, so that plots could be nested in farms and farms in regions.

For three leaf traits, specific leaf area, leaf area and leaf dry matter content, we built trait probability distributions at each scale. The method keeps the variation within a species, fills gaps with a hierarchical procedure, and resamples 200 trait measurements per spatial unit, so a farm distribution reflects the mixture of its plots. From those distributions we computed three standard indices of functional diversity, richness, evenness and divergence, and one further measure: the dissimilarity between the trait space of a farm and the trait space of its region.

Land-use intensity came from farmer interviews, as the average of mowing, grazing and fertilisation over three years and as its variation between the plots of one farm. We modelled each index against scale and management, separately per region, and asked whether farms that depart from the regional average in management also depart from it in their trait space.

## What we found

- **Functional richness grows with the unit of management.** It doubled from the plot to the farm scale across all regions, and increased fourfold from the plot to the regional scale, with no significant further gain from the farm to the region.
- **Evenness and divergence move much less.** Evenness increased by 4% from the plot to the farm, and divergence decreased by 5%; both patterns were clear in Schorfheide-Chorin and Hainich-Dün but not everywhere.
- **Management effects stay weak when plots are pooled.** Hump-shaped responses of functional richness to grazing and fertilisation survived the aggregation to the farm scale in Hainich-Dün and Schwäbische Alb, but their strength was similar to the plot scale and they did not appear when all three regions were modelled together.
- **Varied fertilisation within a farm pays.** A higher coefficient of variation of fertilisation between the plots of one farm was associated with higher functional richness in Hainich-Dün and Schwäbische Alb.- **Ungrazed farms stand out from their region.** Farm-to-regional functional dissimilarity was greatest for farms grazed very little or not at all, and lower for farms managed more intensively than their region.
- **Dissimilarity is tied to farm-level richness.** It correlated strongly and negatively with farm-level functional richness (rho = -0.69), and fell as the number of plots per farm increased (rho = -0.57).

## How this can help

- **A farm-level indicator.** Farm-to-regional functional dissimilarity, read together with farm-level functional richness, flags farms that hold a distinct, complementary set of plant strategies for their region.
- **A check on eco-schemes.** Common Agricultural Policy measures are paid at farm level; an indicator computed at the same level can test whether they change the grassland as intended.
- **Honest expectations about management.** Because grazing and fertilisation effects were weak at every scale, the results warn against promising large shifts in functional diversity from management alone.

## Where the map stops

The analysis is correlative and covers seven years in three regions of one country, on grasslands that are already species-rich. The trait space rests on three leaf traits, so strategies expressed in roots, phenology or dispersal are invisible. The indices describe a farm as a mixture of sampled plots and say nothing about an unsampled field, or about management outside the recorded range.
